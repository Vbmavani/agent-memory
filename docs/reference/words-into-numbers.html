Vectors from zero

How a word turns into numbers

Computers can't compare meanings. They can compare numbers. So the trick behind "vector search" and AI memory is to give every word a short list of numbers, chosen so that words with similar meaning get similar numbers. This page builds that idea up one step at a time, with a tiny made-up example you can check on paper.

Step 1

Start with one number per word

Pick one question, say "how big is it?", and score each word from 0 to 1. Now every word is a single number, and you can put the words on a line. Words close together on the line are similar, at least in size.

One question, one number. A vector with one number in it.

The problem: cat and banana both score 0.2, so with one number they look identical. One number can only describe one thing about a word. The fix is to ask more questions.

Step 2

Two numbers turn the line into a map

Ask two questions instead: "how much is it an animal?" and "how much is it food?". Each word is now a pair of numbers, like cat = [0.9, 0.1]. A pair of numbers is a point on a map, so cat and banana finally land in different places. This pair is what people mean by a vector: a list of numbers.

Tap a word to pick it, then tap a second word to compare. The line between them is their distance: short line, similar meaning.

    Step 3
    
    But who writes the numbers?
    
    
    So far a person picked the questions and the scores. That can't work for 100,000 words. Real systems use one
      simple observation instead: words that appear in the same kind of sentences mean similar things.
      You can guess what "kitten" means without a dictionary, because it shows up where "cat" shows up.
    
    
    
      
    The whole training text for this page: 19 sentences
    
      
      
      
      Little words like the, a, I, is appear everywhere and say nothing, so they are skipped.
      
    
      
    
      Now just count. For each word, count how often it appears in a sentence together with each other word.
      
    
      
      
      
      
      Darker cell = they appear together more often. Read across a row: that row of counts already is a vector for the word.
      
    
      
    
      Rows that look alike belong to words that mean alike. cat and dog both appear with
      chases, drinks, sleeps, sofa. car and bus both appear with drive, fuel, fast. Nobody told the
      computer that cats and dogs are animals. It came out of the counting. The catch: a real vocabulary would make
      each row 100,000 numbers long. Training squeezes that down to a few numbers per word.
      
  
      

  
  
      
    Step 4
    
      Training: start random, then nudge
      
    
      Training starts by giving every word two random numbers, so the map is a mess. Then it reads the sentences
      over and over and repeats one small rule:
      
    
      
      
      
        
      
      
      
      "Pull" means: move the two points a little toward each other. "Push" means: move a random pair a little apart, so everything doesn't collapse into one dot.

            vehiclesother words
          
      
          
      Each pass reads all 19 sentences once. Press Train and watch: nobody tells it which words are
            animals, yet cat, dog and mouse drift together, and so do apple and banana, and car and bus.
      
          
      Look at the axes: after training they have no names. "Number 1" doesn't mean "animal".
            Only the positions relative to each other mean anything.
      
        
      
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
      How the real ones are trained
      
      
      Exactly this pull-and-push idea, scaled up. Word2vec (2013) learned word numbers by guessing which words
        appear next to each other in billions of sentences. Modern embedding models are shown millions of pairs that
        belong together, like a question and its answer or a title and its article, and pull them together while pushing
        unrelated texts apart. Instead of moving points directly, they adjust a neural network, but the goal is the same:
        things that go together end up close.
      
    
      
  
      

  
  
      
    Step 5
    
      What happens when you type a word
      
    
      Training is done once, ahead of time. What's left is a table: one row per word, with that word's numbers.
      When you type a word, nothing clever happens. The computer finds the word's row and reads its numbers.
      
    
      
      
      
        
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
        
      
          
          
      
          
      
          
      
          
      The number table (after training)
      
          
      #wordnumber 1number 2
      
        
      
        
      
      
    
      
    
      For a sentence, the simplest method is to average its words' numbers. The star on the map
      is that average: one point for the whole sentence. Words that aren't in the table are skipped (real systems split
      them into smaller known pieces instead).
      
    
      
      
      One difference in real models
      
      
      Here a word always gets the same numbers. Real embedding models read the whole sentence first, then
        produce the numbers. So "bank" in "river bank" and in "bank account" gets different numbers, because the
        words around it are different. The output is still just a list of numbers, about 1,000 of them.
      
    
      
  
      

  
  
      
    Step 6
    
      Search is "find the nearest point". That's memory.
      
    
      Once anything can become a point, searching by meaning is easy. Save each note together with its point. When a
      question comes in, turn it into a point too and pick the saved notes closest to it. No keyword has to match.
      
    
      
      
      
        
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
        
      
          
      Saved notes
      
          
        
          
          
        
          
        
        
        
        
      
        
    
        
    
        This is how vector memory works in an AI app. Your agent-memory app currently finds old chats by
      matching keywords. With vectors it could find "I'm vegetarian" when you ask "what should I cook?", because the two
      land near each other even though they share no words.

        What to remember: a vector is a list of numbers. Training places words so that words used in similar
      sentences get similar numbers. After that, turning text into numbers is a lookup, and searching by meaning is
      finding the nearest points.
